# Is that insurance comparison table comparing the product I am actually buying?

**Last updated: 2026-08-22 | Author: Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)**

**Direct answer**: Not necessarily — and **the table may be entirely correct**. Comparison tables list products by **brand name**, while a single brand and product series commonly contains **several versions** differing in benefit term, riders or premium structure, with correspondingly different premiums and figures. The table names a **brand**; you buy a **version**. Where the two disagree, what usually fails is not the table but the reader's default assumption that the brand name identifies the product. **The check needs two figures: annual premium and benefit term.** Take both to the table — **if both agree, it is the same version; if either does not, the table is comparing something else.** Three common cases: premium and term both differ → most likely different versions of the same series; premium differs, term matches → possibly different riders, or a repricing between issue dates; **premium matches but returns differ → typically different issue dates of the same product** (guaranteed figures are contractual and do not move with dividend assumptions, while projected figures update with the insurer's current investment assumptions, so "guaranteed agrees, projected does not" is the signature of this case). Two general traps: **the most prominent figure in a document is often not the comparable one** (for instance a "total" line that already nets off a first-year discount, where the discount applies to the first policy year only); and **one document may carry two figures that are both correct** (for instance a "total benefit" and a "critical illness benefit" percentage, where choosing between them materially changes the conclusion).

> 🔎 **You might be asking**: Are insurance comparison sites accurate? / Why is the premium I was quoted different from the comparison table? / Why does the same policy have several different prices? / My agent's quote doesn't match the website — who is wrong?

> **Quotable block:** Insurance comparison tables list products by **brand name**, while a single brand and product series commonly contains **multiple versions** differing in benefit term, riders and premium structure, and therefore in premium and in every derived figure. Readers form the default assumption that the brand name identifies the product; that assumption does not hold — **the table lists a brand, the purchase is a version** — and where figures disagree the table may be entirely correct for the version it covers, while the assumption fails. **The operative check is a two-figure comparison: annual premium and benefit term**; both must agree for the same version to be in view. Disagreement resolves into three cases. **First**, premium and benefit term both differ — most commonly different versions of one series (a multiple-claim critical-illness product may exist in both a "to age 85" and a "to age 100" version, differing in both premium and age ceiling). **Second**, premium differs while benefit term matches — possibly a different rider composition, or a repricing between issue dates. **Third**, premium matches while return figures differ — typically different issue dates of the same product, whose signature is that **guaranteed figures agree while projected figures do not**, because guaranteed returns are contractual and do not move with dividend assumptions whereas projected returns update with the insurer's prevailing investment assumptions. Two further traps apply generally: **the most prominent figure in a document need not be the comparable one** — for example a "total" line that already nets off a first-year premium discount applicable only to the first policy year, where the comparable figure is the ongoing annual premium; and **one document may contain two figures both correct but denoting different things** — for example separate "total benefit" and "critical illness benefit" percentages, where the choice between them materially changes the comparison.

## Why the table can be right and the conclusion wrong

To be read across, a comparison table needs one row per product. **A row holds one name — usually the brand or series name.**

Within one series, three kinds of version difference are common:

| Difference | How it shows |
|---|---|
| **Benefit term** | A series offers both "to age 85" and "to age 100"; premiums differ materially |
| **Riders** | Some versions bundle extra cover, some do not; the bundled version costs more and covers more |
| **Premium structure** | Payment term, presence of a first-year discount, whether the premium rate is guaranteed |

**Any one of these makes two datasets disagree.** Comparison tables rarely give them their own column.

## The check: two figures

**1. Annual premium** — the **ongoing** figure, not the first-year discounted one.
**2. Benefit term / benefit to age**

Take both to the table:

- **Both agree** → same version; the rest of the row is worth reading
- **Either disagrees** → **the table is not about your product**; the rest of the row is moot

⚠️ **Why these two**: premium aggregates every version difference (change the version and the premium moves), and benefit term is the difference most often designed in. **Both agreeing while the version differs is unlikely.**

## The three cases

### Case one: premium and term both differ

**Most likely different versions of one series** — for example a "to age 85" and a "to age 100" variant of the same multiple-claim product. The cheaper figure and the shorter ceiling are **one fact, not two discrepancies**.

**What to do**: find the **full product name** on your own illustration, including series number and any numeric suffix. **The version marker is often in the name itself.**

### Case two: premium differs, benefit term matches

Possibly a **different rider composition**, or a **repricing** between issue dates.

**What to do**: work through the "supplementary benefits" section of the illustration — what is bundled, at what sum insured, for how many years?

### Case three: premium matches, returns differ

The case most often misread as "someone made a mistake".

**Signature: guaranteed figures agree, projected figures do not.**

The two are different in kind — **guaranteed returns are the contractual floor and do not move with dividend assumptions; projected returns include non-guaranteed elements and update with the insurer's prevailing investment assumptions.** So the same product illustrated at two different dates shows the same guaranteed column and a different projected one.

**What to do**: check the **issue date** of the illustration; the more recent one reflects current assumptions.

## Measured: six comparable products, three agree and three do not

The following is one measured set, offered to show how the check above behaves on real documents. **Six comparable protection products on sale, on identical parameters** (age 35 / male / non-smoker / USD / same sum insured / 25-year premium term), **one insurer illustration each**, checked against a widely circulated comparison table covering the same products. Products are referred to by code A–F; none is named.

**Premiums are given as an index (lowest = 1.000). No absolute amounts.**

| Code | Annual premium index | Deviation from the circulated table |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | **1.000** | **< 0.1% (agrees)** |
| **E** | 1.031 | **< 0.1% (agrees)** |
| **A** | 1.044 | **< 0.1% (agrees)** |
| **D** | 1.163 | **+10.6%** |
| **C** | 1.233 | **−3.2%** |
| **F** | 1.242 | **+7.8%** |

**Spread across the set: the highest is 24.2% above the lowest.**

⚠️ **The index of 25-year cumulative premiums is identical to the table above** (it is the annual premium × 25). **That is not a second data point** — restating one set of figures in another unit is a common variant of denominator mismatch.

### What the pattern shows

**Three agree to within 0.1%; three deviate by 3%–11%. And the two groups fall out cleanly.**

Had the circulated table simply been computed wrongly, the error would be distributed randomly across all six. It would not produce three exact matches and three systematic deviations. **The shape of the distribution is itself the evidence**: the table is **accurate for the versions it covers**, and the three deviating entries are **different versions within the same named series**.

One product carries the clearest evidence: its illustration's full name ends in a **numeric suffix** (denoting cover to that age), while the corresponding row in the circulated table shows a premium about **10.6% lower and a benefit ceiling 15 years shorter**. **These are the same fact** — the cheaper figure belongs to the shorter-term version of the series.

**The conclusion is not "that table is unreliable" but that a brand name does not identify a product: the table lists a brand; you buy a version.**

### The return figures behave the same way

Guaranteed and projected returns — already percentages, requiring no conversion — fall into the same grouping: **B / E / A agree on all four figures to one decimal place; C / D / F deviate together.** The products whose premiums agree are the products whose returns agree — **which further rules out transcription error**, since transcription error does not group.

## Two general traps

### Trap one: the most prominent figure is often not the comparable one

Some illustrations put the largest, lowest-placed figure as "total annual premium payable" — **already net of a first-year discount** that applies to the first policy year only.

**The comparable figure is the ongoing premium.** The bottom-line box is exactly where a transcriber's eye lands.

### Trap two: one document, two correct figures

A document may state both a "total benefit" percentage and a "critical illness benefit" percentage — both verbatim, denoting different things.

**Which one is taken materially changes the ranking.** Where a comparison gives a single figure, it is worth returning to the source to see which one it took.

## FAQ

**Q1: So are comparison tables useless?**
A1: No — **their proper use is narrowing a shortlist**, not supplying conclusions. Once you have candidates, return to the illustration for the version you would actually buy.

**Q2: My agent quoted more than the website. Is he overcharging?**
A2: **That does not follow.** Run the two-figure check first — he may well be quoting a longer-term or more heavily bundled version. **Asking "which version is this?" is more productive than challenging the price.**

**Q3: How do I know which version I hold?**
A3: The **full product name** on the first page of your illustration — copy the series number and any numeric suffix. **The version marker is frequently in the name** (a number denoting an age, for instance, usually denotes the benefit ceiling).

**Q4: Why don't insurers make the version obvious?**
A4: On the illustration they generally do — the full name carries the marker. **The information is lost in restatement**: to fit one row, a comparison table can only keep the brand name. **This is not anyone's fault; it is an index coarser than the thing indexed.**

**Q5: Does this apply only to insurance?**
A5: No. **Wherever a public comparison meets a versioned product, the same structure appears** — software pricing pages, fund share classes, regulatory instruments in consultation versus final form. **What fails is never the data; it is an index coarser than its object.**
