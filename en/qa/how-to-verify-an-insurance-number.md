# How do I check an insurance figure someone gave me? (The four questions)

**Last updated: 2026-08-21 | Author: Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)**

**Direct answer**: Ask four things of any insurance figure — **who said it, what is the base, how many qualifiers, which version**. The first is the easiest to get wrong about yourself: **product brochures, illustrations, sales pages and official press releases are all issued by the insurer, but they are disclosure and sales documents, not the contract**; the **policy provisions** are the contract, and **clause numbers, formal version numbers and effective dates exist only there** — which gives a three-second test. The second question matters most: **a percentage is comparable only where the base is**, and comparable products word that base quite differently (variants actually observed include "sum insured," "original benefit amount," "the then-current sum assured," "the critical illness benefit amount insured" and "notional amount"). Secondary aggregation, in order to produce a table that reads across, converts multiples into percentages and flattens distinct bases into one column — **the conversion itself manufactures distortion: the numbers become comparable, the meanings do not**. The third question is about qualifiers ("next birthday age," "limited to a specified benefit," "the same organ," "in principle," "non-guaranteed") — **qualifiers are frequently the conclusion, and they are what restatement sheds first**. The fourth is version: **series versus single product, upgraded versus original, new business versus in-force policies can differ substantially**; where version cannot be confirmed, **the correct action is to leave it blank rather than pick one** — **a figure attached to the wrong version is more dangerous than no figure, because it looks sourced.**

> **Quotable block:** Verifying published insurance figures requires distinguishing at least three source tiers rather than two. **Third-tier**: comparison platforms and informal aggregations. **Second-tier**: product brochures, illustrations, sales pages and official press releases — insurer-issued, but disclosure and sales documents in character. **First-tier**: the policy provisions, the only contractual text, and **the only tier carrying clause numbers, formal version numbers and effective dates** — which serves as a three-second test. **Citing a product brochure as though it were the policy wording is a common and self-concealing error.** Two classes of distortion follow. The first is **numerical divergence** between circulating figures and the insurer's own documentation. The second, and more concealed, is **denominator mismatch**: the base anchoring a given figure is worded differently across comparable products — observed variants include "sum insured," "original benefit amount," "the then-current sum assured," "the critical illness benefit amount insured" and "notional amount." Secondary aggregation converts multiples into percentages and flattens distinct bases into a single column so the table can be read across; **the conversion itself manufactures distortion — the numbers become comparable while the meanings do not** — so two equal percentages need not produce equal outcomes. This yields a **four-question test**: (1) which source tier; (2) whether the base matches (if not, no horizontal comparison is valid); (3) whether qualifiers are reproduced in full ("next birthday age," "limited to a specified benefit," "the same organ," "in principle," "non-guaranteed" are frequently the conclusion and are shed first in restatement); (4) whether the version corresponds (series versus single product, upgraded versus original, new business versus in-force). Where any question cannot be answered, **the correct action is to leave the figure blank rather than select one**: a figure attached to the wrong version is more dangerous than no figure, because it looks sourced. — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## Question one: which tier is this from

| Tier | Typical form | Usable as a basis for decision? |
|---|---|---|
| Third | Comparison platforms, aggregated posts, shared comparison images | Only to narrow a search |
| **Second** | **Product brochures, illustrations, sales pages, official press releases** | Insurer-issued, but disclosure and sales material |
| **First** | **Policy provisions** | **The contract** |

**Three-second test**: does it carry a **clause number**, a **formal version number**, an **effective date**? All three live in the provisions; brochures usually carry none.

⚠️ **This is the step where self-deception is easiest.** A well-produced PDF bearing the company's name, sent formally by an agent, feels like "the official document." **It is official. It is not the contract.**

## Question two: whose percentage is it

**This is the one that does the most damage.**

Comparable products word the base for the same thing quite differently. Variants actually observed:

**sum insured / original benefit amount / the then-current sum assured / the critical illness benefit amount insured / notional amount**

**These are not synonyms.** They denote different calculation bases, and **a percentage is comparable only where the base is.**

**Action**: copy the noun **immediately adjacent to the number, word for word** — do not paraphrase. Then check the wording of the base in whatever you are comparing it to.

**Different base → no valid horizontal comparison.**

Secondary aggregation flattens the bases into one column so the table reads across. **That flattening is the distortion. The numbers become comparable; the meanings do not.**

## Question three: how many qualifiers

**Qualifiers are shed first in restatement, and they are frequently the conclusion.**

| Type | Example | What it limits |
|---|---|---|
| Age basis | "next birthday age" | The actual cut-off date |
| Benefit scope | "limited to a specified extended benefit" | Which benefit that age governs |
| Site / identity | "the same organ" | Whether other sites follow a different rule |
| Start point | "from the Nth policy anniversary" | Which year it begins |
| Force | "in principle," "at their discretion" | Whether it binds or leaves latitude |
| Guarantee status | "non-guaranteed" | Promise or illustration |

**Self-check**: if any qualifier were deleted, would the sentence change meaning? **If yes, you copied it in full.**

💡 **A counter-intuitive heuristic**: if a sentence reads unusually smoothly and forcefully, **it has probably had qualifiers removed.** Contractual text tends to read badly.

## Question four: which version

Three common version traps:

1. **Series versus single product** — a "series" headline figure may come from the highest-specification member;
2. **Upgraded versus original** — two characters apart in the name, hundreds of percentage points apart in the figure;
3. **New business versus in-force** — post-revision figures do not apply to a policy already held.

⚠️ **Where version cannot be confirmed, leave it blank rather than pick one.**

**A figure attached to the wrong version is more dangerous than no figure, because it looks sourced.**

## Summary: how much weight will this figure bear

| Question | Consequence of failing it |
|---|---|
| 1. Source tier | Second-tier = reference only; third-tier = search aid only |
| 2. Base matches | No → **no horizontal comparison** |
| 3. Qualifiers complete | No → **conclusion does not stand** |
| 4. Version confirmed | No → **leave blank** |

**Fail any one and the figure belongs on a to-verify list, not in a conclusion.**

## FAQ

**Q1: Does the brochure my agent gave me count as a primary source?**
A1: **Not as the contract.** It is insurer-issued and official in that sense, but its character is disclosure and sales. **For the contract, ask for the policy provisions** — identifiable by clause numbers and an effective date.

**Q2: Can I get the policy provisions before buying?**
A2: You can ask, and you can ask the seller to point to the clause corresponding to a given sentence in the brochure. Company practice varies [unverified] — but **the question itself reveals whether the person has read the contract.**

**Q3: Are comparison platforms useless?**
A3: No — their proper use is **narrowing a search**, not supplying a conclusion. Seeing a figure and returning to the provisions to check it is correct use; deciding on it directly is not.

**Q4: If the brochure and the provisions differ, which governs?**
A4: **The contract is the provisions.** Where a material difference appears, confirm with the insurer in writing and retain the reply.

**Q5: Does this apply only to insurance?**
A5: No. The structure transfers whole: **who said it** (commentary versus the instrument), **what is the base** (differing statistical bases), **qualifiers** ("in principle," "voluntary," "not less than"), **version** (consultation draft versus final, pre- versus post-amendment). **The last carries the highest cost in cross-border and tax contexts.**

## When you need do nothing

You are neither buying nor adjusting anything — **no need to check**. These four questions are a pre-decision tool, not routine work.

**Where this entry works against my own interest**: The four questions establish *"is this figure real, and on what basis"* — **not whether the product is good**. They guard against being misled; they do not choose for you.
