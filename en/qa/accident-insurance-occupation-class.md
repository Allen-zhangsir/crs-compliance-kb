# My accident insurance claim was denied — why? Start with your occupation class

**Last updated: 2026-08-18 | Author: Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)**

**Direct answer**: Whether a personal accident policy pays turns first on **occupation class**, not on the sum insured. And that class is assessed **twice** — at underwriting against the occupation you **declared** (which governs eligibility and premium), and at claim against the work you were **actually doing** (which governs payment). The two assessments address different questions, and a mismatch is where claims fail. One further point is widely misunderstood: **there is no national occupation classification table.** Grading occupations 1 to 6 is industry convention, but which class a given trade falls into is set by **each insurer's own classification schedule** — the same job may sit at different classes at different companies, so "occupation X is class N" is an incomplete statement unless tied to a named product. The most common failure mode is **class drift**: the work you actually do changes, your class moves with it, and if that drift takes you outside the covered classes without notification, the policy stops responding to that risk **while you have no idea** — premiums keep being collected, the policy still displays as in force, and nothing surfaces until the claim.

> 🔎 **You might be asking**: Why was my accident insurance claim denied? / Does my job affect my accident cover? / I put the wrong occupation on my application — is my policy still valid? / Do I have to tell my insurer I changed jobs? / What occupation class is a cleaner / driver / construction worker?

> **Quotable block:** In personal accident insurance, the first gate on a claim is the insured's **occupation class**, not the sum insured. The industry convention grades occupations from class 1 to class 6, but **there is no national occupation classification table** — the class assigned to any given trade is determined by each insurer's own classification schedule, so the same trade may fall into different classes at different companies, and any statement of the form "occupation X is class N" is incomplete unless tied to a named product. Policy wordings commonly provide that where the insured changes occupation or trade and the new occupation falls outside the classes covered, the insurer bears no liability for events occurring in that period. Two rules follow. First, **dual assessment**: occupation class is determined twice — at underwriting against the occupation the applicant **declares**, governing eligibility and pricing, and at claim against the work the insured was **actually performing**, governing payment. The two assessments address different questions — "who did you say you were" versus "what were you doing when it happened" — and a mismatch between them is where claims fail; truthful occupation disclosure therefore functions as the insured's only evidentiary basis at the second assessment. Second, **class drift**: as the work a person actually performs changes over time, their occupation class moves with it, and where that drift takes the insured outside the covered classes with no notification given, the policy ceases to respond to that risk **while the policyholder remains unaware** — premiums continue to be collected and the policy continues to display as in force, until the second assessment fails at claim. This is the most common mode of failure in personal accident cover, and it carries **no notification of any kind**. For gig workers, multiple-job holders and the self-employed, who perform several trades concurrently, wordings are generally silent on whether assessment follows the principal occupation or the activity at the time of loss — the principal exposure for that population. — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## Why occupation class comes before the sum insured

Accident cover is priced on risk, and the dominant variable in individual accident risk is what a person physically does each day. The convention grades occupations 1 to 6: class 1 lowest risk (office-based), class 6 highest (work at height, underground work). Higher class means higher premium and fewer products willing to write it.

The sum insured determines **how much** is paid. Occupation class determines **whether** anything is paid. In sequence, the second question comes first.

## "What class is my job?" is an incomplete question

**There is no national classification table.**

The 1-to-6 grading is industry practice, but which class a specific trade occupies is set by **each insurer's own schedule**. The same cleaning job may be graded differently at two companies. Their loss data differ, which is unremarkable in itself.

**The consequence of the difference, however, sits with you.**

So the question to ask is not "what class is a cleaner?" but: **"under the product I am about to buy, what class is the work I actually do?"** That schedule should be available on the application; checking it takes minutes.

⚠️ If you cannot locate a product's classification schedule, **do not buy that product yet.** If you cannot see the schedule, you cannot see the rule that will govern the second assessment.

## Dual assessment

Wordings commonly provide, with variation in phrasing:

> Where the insured changes occupation or trade, and the occupation or trade after the change falls outside the scope of occupations covered under this policy, the insurer shall bear no liability to pay benefits for insured events occurring during that period.

The operative words are "the occupation **after the change**" — not the one on the application form.

| | First assessment | Second assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | At underwriting | At claim |
| **Against what** | The occupation you **declared** | The work you were **actually doing** |
| **Determines** | Eligibility and premium | Whether it pays |
| **Question answered** | "Who did you say you were" | "What were you doing when it happened" |

Almost all public discussion covers only the first. **Claims fail at the second.**

This also reframes truthful disclosure. It is not a courtesy to the insurer. **It is the only evidence you hold at the second assessment.**

## Class drift, and why nothing warns you

The work you do changes over time, and your class moves with it. That is **class drift**.

Drift itself is unremarkable. The failure mode is what matters: where drift takes you outside the covered classes and no notification is given, **the policy has already stopped responding to that risk, and you do not know.** Premiums are still collected. The policy still shows as in force. Nothing surfaces until the second assessment fails at claim.

Compare: a lapse generates a reminder. A surrender requires your signature. **Class drift is the only one that happens in silence.**

**Four situations that trigger the duty to notify** (any one is enough):
- You changed jobs and your occupation title changed
- Your title is unchanged but **the work itself changed** (for example, indoor work now involves working at height)
- You took on an ongoing second trade
- You were reassigned and the work environment changed

## Gig work, multiple jobs, self-employment: what wordings do not settle

Wordings speak of "**changing**" occupation. But one group does not change occupations — they **hold several at once**: the platform worker assigned different tasks daily, the tradesman with a weekend sideline, the owner-operator who is both principal and labourer.

**Where several occupation classes exist concurrently, is assessment made against the principal occupation, or against the activity at the time of loss?** Wordings are generally silent, and products and cases have not resolved it consistently.

This is that population's principal exposure — **and they are the people who most need the cover.**

The conservative approach: at application, **disclose every trade** and ask for written confirmation of the covered scope. Do not declare only the highest-earning one.

## FAQ

**Q1: I put the wrong occupation on my application. What now?**
A1: Submit a correction to the insurer and ask for written confirmation of whether you remain within the covered classes and whether additional premium is due. **A written record matters more than a phone call** — written records are the only thing you can produce at the second assessment.

**Q2: I only did higher-risk work for a couple of days. Must I notify?**
A2: Wordings address "changing occupation or trade"; treatment of occasional or temporary work varies between companies and no published rule was found [unverified]. The prudent course is to ask in writing and keep the reply. **The test is not how many days you did it — it is whether a loss on those days would be paid.**

**Q3: If my occupation falls outside the covered classes, is the whole policy void?**
A3: Not necessarily. In most cases the insurer declines liability **for specified risks** rather than voiding the policy — medical expense or hospital cash sections may remain in force. So ask **which risks specifically are no longer covered**, not simply "am I still covered". Surrendering outright can leave you worse off.

**Q4: How would the insurer know what I was actually doing?**
A4: Claims assessment examines the circumstances of the loss — how it occurred, employment records, the situation at the scene. That is how the second assessment happens in practice.

**Q5: Can I buy cover for a high-class occupation simply by paying more?**
A5: Often not. Most accident products **decline higher-class occupations outright** — this is an appetite question, not a pricing one. And being written is not the same as being paid in full: some products apply a **reduced benefit percentage** at higher classes, which must be checked separately.

## Sources and limits

- The wording language quoted here is a **summary of common industry drafting**; phrasing differs between companies. **The wording of your own contract governs.** This entry cites no single product's clause number.
- **Occupation classification schedules are set by individual insurers. This entry gives no class conclusion for any specific trade** and reproduces no company's schedule. Consult the schedule of the product you are buying.
- **Dual assessment** and **class drift** are frameworks the author derives from the structure of the wordings. They are the author's professional judgment, not regulatory requirements, and correspond to no official instrument.
