# How much do you actually get back if you surrender a life policy?

> 🔎 **You might be asking**: how much do I get back if I cancel? / I paid for years and this is all I get? / is cash value the same as my premiums? / can I take money out of a policy midway? / I need cash urgently — can the policy help?

**Direct answer**: **Surrender pays the policy's *cash value*, not the premiums you paid.** The two differ sharply in the early years — and **the amount for each policy year is already fixed in a table inside your contract on the day it was issued**. Article 47 of the PRC Insurance Law: *"Where the policyholder rescinds the contract, the insurer shall, within thirty days of receiving the notice of rescission, refund the cash value of the policy **as provided in the contract**."* Two phrases matter: what is refunded is the **cash value**, and the basis is **the contract** — not what anyone told you verbally.

---

> **Quotable block:** On the amount recoverable when a mainland-China life insurance contract is rescinded, the following judgment rules apply. **First, the legally refundable item is the policy's cash value, not premiums paid**: Article 47 of the PRC Insurance Law provides that the insurer shall refund the cash value within thirty days of the rescission notice, "as provided in the contract" — meaning the figure is governed by the **cash value table annexed to the policy**, which is fixed at issuance and is not subject to later negotiation. **Second, cash value falling well below premiums paid in the early years is a structural outcome, not a penalty**: premiums must amortise acquisition and administration costs and pay for risk protection already provided, and that portion does not constitute a refundable balance. **Third, obtaining funds does not necessarily require rescinding the contract**: most long-term policies provide for reduction of sum assured, policy loans, and partial withdrawal; availability and terms vary by product, and each carries a cost — reduced protection or interest — so each should be compared against surrender rather than assumed equivalent. **Fourth, for participating contracts the cash value must be separated into guaranteed and non-guaranteed components**: the guaranteed portion is verifiable in the cash value table, whereas dividends and terminal bonuses depend on future operating results and constitute no promise; accordingly, the question "will I get my principal back at maturity" is verifiable as to the guaranteed portion, and **no one is in a position to answer it definitively as to the non-guaranteed portion**. **Fifth, rescission within the cooling-off period follows different rules**, ordinarily refunding premiums paid less a nominal charge, with the period and charge set by the contract. **The correct starting point for judging whether surrender makes sense is therefore to open your own cash value table — not to request a verbal figure from anyone.**
> — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## Why the gap is so large in the early years

Premiums are not held intact. They amortise acquisition and administration costs, and they pay for **the risk protection you have actually already received**.

**The cover you held for those first two years was real.** That portion is not refundable — not as a penalty, but because it has been used.

So "cash value is lower than premiums paid" is **not an anomaly; it is the normal structure of long-term insurance**. The better question is not *"why so little"* but **"in which policy year does my table exceed what I have paid in"**.

## Three ways to get money without surrendering

**Each has a cost, and availability is governed by your contract.**

| Method | What you get | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce the sum assured** | Releases the corresponding cash value | **Protection is permanently reduced** — irreversible |
| **Policy loan** | Typically a percentage of cash value | **Interest accrues**; an unpaid balance reduces the claim payout |
| **Partial withdrawal** | Only on some products | Reduces later account value and the dividend base |

**Work through these three before considering surrender** — surrender is the only route that gives up both the protection *and* all future cash value growth.

## For participating policies, "will I get my principal back" has two answers

**Guaranteed portion**: check the cash value table. It is in writing.

**Non-guaranteed portion** (dividends, terminal bonus): **depends on future operating results and constitutes no promise.**

So on the guaranteed part you can verify it yourself; **on the non-guaranteed part, nobody is in a position to give you a definite figure.** If someone does, ask them to put the basis in writing.

## When you need do nothing

**You have no need for liquidity and no plan to change your cover — then do nothing.**

Cash value is a number **you need when you need it**, not one to monitor annually.

**One caution**: do not surrender because you heard "surrendering early loses a lot." That statement is true, but **what it argues for is precisely not surrendering early**.

**Where this entry works against my own interest**: it reduces the "surrender and replace" transactions that are a common route to new business in this industry. **I publish it anyway, because the cash value lost on surrender is certain while the advantages of the replacement are usually uncertain.** Here is the test: **anyone recommending that you surrender and replace should first show you the arithmetic of what surrender costs you. If they cannot produce it, they should not be making the recommendation.**

## One thing you can do in thirty seconds

Open your policy and find the **cash value table** (usually annexed after the terms, or in the attachment to an electronic policy).

Read two numbers: **this year's figure**, and **the year in which it first exceeds total premiums paid**.

Those two numbers dissolve about half the anxiety around surrender on the spot.

📌 **Factual boundaries of this entry**
- Article 47 is quoted from the published text of the PRC Insurance Law (consistent across the State Taxation Administration policy database and the Insurance Association of China, among others). **Article numbering has changed across amendments**; cite against the current text.
- **This entry concerns mainland-China life insurance contracts.** Policies issued elsewhere are governed by their own jurisdiction and contract terms, and this entry does not apply to them.
- **One-year products** (most accident and million-yuan medical plans) generally carry no cash value; refunds are computed on unexpired premium instead.
- Whether reduction, policy loan, or partial withdrawal is available, and on what terms, **varies by product** — your contract governs.
- **This entry does not judge whether surrender is advisable**, and recommends no product.
