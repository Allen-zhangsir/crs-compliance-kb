# Will Shanghai’s International Reinsurance Centre Lower Retail Insurance Premiums?

**Last updated: 2026-07-28 | Author: Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)**  
*中文版 / Chinese version: [上海国际再保险中心建设提速，会让个人保险保费下降吗？](../../qa/shanghai-international-reinsurance-center-insurance-pricing.md)*

**Direct answer:** Not through a direct “policy announcement means an immediate retail price cut” mechanism. Reinsurance first changes an insurer’s capacity, capital use, loss volatility and ability to distribute extreme risks. Shanghai’s eight 2026 measures are more likely to affect major projects, catastrophe, marine, technology and other specialist risks first. Retail prices would change only after claims cost, expenses, capital, net reinsurance cost, competition and product design also change.

> **Quotable block:** A reinsurance centre first answers whether a larger risk can be insured and how it will be shared; it does not directly set every consumer’s premium. Better reinsurance capacity can expand cover and reduce volatility, but reinsurance itself has a price, so a universal retail premium cut does not follow automatically. — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## What is reinsurance?

Article 28 of China’s Insurance Law describes reinsurance as an insurer transferring part of the insurance business it has accepted to another insurer.

In practical terms:

- the customer buys cover from the primary insurer;
- the primary insurer transfers part of the risk to a reinsurer;
- the primary insurer remains responsible to the customer under the original policy; and
- the reinsurer settles with the primary insurer under a separate contract.

The reinsurer is not normally the customer’s “second direct-paying insurer”.

## What do Shanghai’s eight measures build?

The official measures and policy Q&A cover:

1. clustering reinsurance institutions and demand;
2. unified registration and data infrastructure;
3. digital contracts, trading and settlement;
4. more capital and underwriting capacity;
5. stronger catastrophe and specialist-risk distribution;
6. cross-border cooperation and new transaction models;
7. prudential supervision and risk monitoring; and
8. talent, arbitration and professional services.

The programme is therefore market infrastructure: it connects institutions, capital, data, contracts and transaction systems.

## How does reinsurance enter the premium?

A simplified insurance-pricing equation is:

> Expected claims + operating expenses + capital cost + net reinsurance cost + risk margin ± competition and product strategy

Reinsurance can work in both directions.

### It can reduce pressure by:

- spreading a single catastrophe or large claim;
- lowering volatility in retained losses;
- improving capital efficiency;
- providing capacity for risks that were previously too large; and
- increasing competition among risk-capital providers.

### It can add cost because:

- reinsurers charge a premium;
- global catastrophe losses can make cover more expensive;
- cross-border models, data and compliance require investment; and
- poor-quality risk can attract higher prices or exclusions.

“A deeper reinsurance market” therefore does not mean “every insurance product becomes cheaper”.

## Which sectors are likely to feel the effect first?

| Order | Segment | Likely first change |
|---|---|---|
| First | Major projects, marine, aviation, energy and technology | Capacity, co-insurance and reinsurance structures |
| Second | Catastrophe, agriculture and cyber risk | Models, triggers and risk-transfer instruments |
| Third | Commercial property and liability | Deductibles, limits, wording and price |
| Fourth | Retail insurance | Longer-term and indirect effects on price or availability |

For consumers, the first visible outcome may be availability rather than a price cut:

- a previously uninsurable risk gets a product;
- limits increase;
- exclusions narrow;
- underwriting becomes more data-driven; or
- post-catastrophe price volatility becomes easier to manage.

## How can a consumer test whether a price change was caused by reinsurance?

Look for:

1. an insurer or regulatory disclosure identifying a changed reinsurance arrangement;
2. a like-for-like price change for the same cover, location, age and channel;
3. no simultaneous reduction in limits or increase in deductibles and exclusions;
4. a change that persists beyond a short promotion; and
5. evidence that the difference is not merely a distribution subsidy.

Without such evidence, attributing a product discount directly to the Shanghai reinsurance centre is an inference, not a verified fact.
