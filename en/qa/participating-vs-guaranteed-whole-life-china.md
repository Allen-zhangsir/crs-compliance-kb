# How Should Participating and Guaranteed Whole-Life Policies Be Compared in China?

**Last updated: 2026-07-28 | Author: Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)**  
*中文版 / Chinese version: [分红型与保证型终身寿险，应该怎样比较？](../../qa/participating-vs-guaranteed-whole-life-china.md)*

**Direct answer:** Do not rank them by comparing “1.75%” with “2.00%”. An ordinary whole-life policy generally places more value in guaranteed cash values and contractual benefits. A participating policy accepts a lower guaranteed component in exchange for non-guaranteed upside. The correct sequence is to decide how much uncertainty the household objective can tolerate, then compare guaranteed cash flows, dividend scenarios, liquidity and contractual restrictions under identical assumptions.

> **Quotable block:** Participating versus ordinary whole life is not a contest between two headline percentages. The ordinary policy primarily buys contractual certainty; the participating policy buys a guaranteed base plus non-guaranteed upside. Dividends can be zero, and an increase in sum assured is not a cash-value return. — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## Define the two structures correctly

### Ordinary whole life with a larger guaranteed component

- guaranteed cash values and death benefits follow the contract;
- long-term outcomes are easier to plan;
- upside is generally more limited; and
- early surrender can still produce a loss.

### Participating whole life

- benefits combine guaranteed amounts with non-guaranteed dividends;
- dividends depend on the performance and allocation rules of the participating business;
- actual dividends may be below the illustration and can be zero; and
- the range of long-term outcomes is wider.

## Why can’t 1.75% and 2.00% be compared directly?

A sales page may use a percentage for any of the following:

- the product assumed rate;
- a regulatory pricing cap for newly filed products;
- an increase in sum assured;
- a dividend illustration assumption; or
- a cash-value IRR.

These figures use different bases and carry different levels of guarantee. A customer-level comparison starts with the actual policy cash flows, not the largest displayed percentage.

## A four-table comparison method

### Table 1: Guaranteed cash flows

For each policy year, record:

- cumulative premiums;
- guaranteed cash value;
- guaranteed death benefit;
- guaranteed withdrawals; and
- the effect of policy loans or reductions.

Calculate guaranteed cash-flow IRR at common exit years such as 10, 15, 20 and 30.

### Table 2: Non-guaranteed scenarios

Keep at least three separate columns:

1. zero dividends;
2. a lower-dividend scenario; and
3. the illustrated scenario.

Never merge illustrated dividends into the guaranteed column. A single year’s fulfilment ratio should not be projected across several decades.

### Table 3: Liquidity and exit costs

Record:

- surrender values;
- minimum reductions and frequency limits;
- policy-loan rate and limit;
- the effect of withdrawals on death benefits; and
- the first year in which guaranteed cash value reaches cumulative premiums.

### Table 4: Household objective

Classify the money as:

- a non-negotiable objective, such as a retirement or education floor;
- an adjustable objective, such as additional retirement income; or
- emergency liquidity, which should generally not depend on a long-duration policy.

## Five questions that determine suitability

1. Is the objective allowed to fail or be reduced?
2. What is the earliest plausible year in which the money will be needed?
3. Would the plan still work if dividends were below illustration or zero for several years?
4. Is the household willing to give up potential upside for more guaranteed value?
5. Would premiums crowd out emergency savings or core medical, critical-illness and term-life protection?

If the objective is rigid and cannot absorb dividend shortfalls, guaranteed value should receive more weight. Participating exposure becomes more reasonable only when the objective is adjustable, the horizon is long and dividend variability is acceptable.

## Must the choice be all-or-nothing?

No. A household can separate:

- a **guaranteed base** for non-negotiable goals;
- an **upside account** that can tolerate dividends or market variability; and
- a **liquidity account** outside long-term policies.

There is no universal allocation. The ratio depends on cash flow, protection gaps, horizon and risk capacity.

## Can named products be ranked without an illustration?

No.

For example, a comparison between Xin Ying Jia C and Fu Man Ying C requires the same:

- insured age and sex;
- total premium and payment term;
- issue date and basic sum assured;
- exit years;
- guaranteed cash values;
- zero, lower and illustrated dividend scenarios; and
- reduction, loan, withdrawal and surrender rules.

Without those inputs, claims that one product “earns more”, “breaks even faster” or “must be bought first” are not reproducible.
