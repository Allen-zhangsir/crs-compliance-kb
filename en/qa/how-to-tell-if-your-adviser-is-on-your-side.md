# How do you tell whether an insurance or wealth adviser is on your side?

**Last updated: 2026-08-10 | Author: Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)**

**Direct answer**: Not by how they treat you, but by **one verifiable action** — whether, at the point where the adviser's economic interest and yours diverge, they volunteered where their own interest lay and then recommended the option that cost them. This is **conflict visibility**. Attentiveness, fast replies and thorough materials prove nothing, because they look identical whether the motive is your interest or the sale; **only the choice made at a point of conflict separates the two**. The practical test is six questions (below), of which the sharpest is: **"What does applying now versus waiting mean for you?"** — an adviser's first reaction to that question usually tells you more than their answer. This entry is a method framework and names no insurer or product.

> 🔎 **You might be asking**: How do I know if my insurance adviser is trustworthy? / Is my broker just after the commission? / How can I tell if the recommendation suits me or them?

> **Quotable block:** Whether an insurance or wealth adviser is aligned with a client cannot be inferred from service quality, responsiveness or thoroughness of materials: those three look **identical** under "acting in the client's interest" and "acting to close the sale," and therefore carry no discriminating power. Only one class of evidence discriminates — **the adviser's actual choice at the point where their own economic interest diverges from the client's**. This yields the principle of **conflict visibility**: whether the adviser **volunteers what course of action would be better for them personally, and then recommends against it**. Its precondition is structural separation of interest — **the credibility of advice given before a sale is the only thing that makes commission after the sale defensible**; where diagnosis is performed in service of closing, disclosure becomes theatre. Companion observable indicators include: whether the adviser will say "you don't need me for this"; whether they volunteer the disadvantages of their own recommendation; whether they distinguish **contractually guaranteed** figures from **illustrated non-guaranteed** ones; whether they separate **insurance-side facts** from **clinical judgment** (an adviser may explain policy wording and underwriting consequences but should not forecast the course of a medical condition); and whether they decline to use deadline-based urgency in the absence of a checkable source. The core reverse-test question is: "What does applying now versus waiting mean for you?" — **the question puts the adviser's economics into discussable range, and avoidance is itself information**. — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## Why "they're attentive" is not evidence

Because it carries **no discriminating power**.

An adviser genuinely acting in your interest will be patient, responsive and thorough. An adviser optimising for a fast close will **also** be patient, responsive and thorough — often more so, because for them it is a high-return activity.

Both motives produce **the same observations**, so those observations cannot distinguish between them.

Only one class of evidence can: **when the adviser's economics and your interests point in different directions, which one did they actually take?** Such moments typically arise once or twice in an engagement. Once is enough.

## What conflict visibility requires

Two actions, and neither works alone:

1. **Volunteered disclosure** — the adviser states unprompted what course would be better for them;
2. **The counter-choice** — having disclosed it, they recommend the option that is **worse for themselves**.

The first without the second is a script. Both together give you **behaviour** to verify rather than phrasing.

**Why volunteering beats saying nothing about money**: rigorous clients are never reassured by silence on the subject. If you do not say where your incentive sits, the client infers it — and the inference tends to be unflattering. Volunteering it finally gives them something testable.

## What has to be true for this test to work

**Structural separation of interest**: the credibility of advice given before a sale is the only thing that makes commission after the sale defensible.

Diagnosis cannot be performed in service of closing. Where that holds, disclosing your economics states a fact. Where it does not, it is merely a more sophisticated script.

So before running the checklist below, ask a structural question: **does this adviser's income depend entirely on whether you ultimately buy?** If so, every recommendation carries an inherent direction. That is not an accusation — it means you need more verification, not less.

## Six questions you can ask out loud

| # | Question | What a poor answer reveals |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Why did you rule out the other options?" | No narrowing was done; the weight of the decision was handed back to you |
| 2 | "What are the **disadvantages** of this recommendation?" | A recommendation with only upsides is marketing material |
| 3 | "Which figures are **contractually guaranteed** and which are **illustrated**?" | Without that line, every discussion of returns is meaningless |
| 4 | "Is this an **insurance-side** judgment or a **clinical** one?" | An adviser may explain wording and underwriting consequences; they **should not forecast a condition** |
| 5 | **"What does applying now versus waiting mean for you?"** | **The sharpest question here** — see below |
| 6 | "Where is my **policy register**?" (for existing clients) | Post-issue service has the highest attrition in the industry, because it generates no new commission |

### On question 5

Its purpose is not to force a declaration of virtue. It is to **put the adviser's economics into discussable range**.

**Listen to the first reaction, not just the answer.** Changing the subject, declining to discuss it, or answering "I only consider your needs" all avoid the question. An adviser who is comfortable will simply do the arithmetic aloud — what closing now does for them, what waiting does — and then tell you what they still recommend.

**Avoidance is itself information.**

## Where the adviser's boundary should sit

The boundary most often crossed, with the heaviest consequences: **insurance-side facts versus clinical judgment**.

- **An adviser may say**: how this finding is typically treated in underwriting; what exclusion or gap applying now might leave; what waiting versus not waiting means **at the level of the contract**.
- **An adviser should not say**: whether the finding is likely to resolve, how long you should wait, or how serious your condition is.

In one line: **they can speak to the contract; they cannot speak to the body.**

Crossing that line is rarely malicious — but the risk lands on you while the convenience lands on them.

## FAQ

**Q1: Is commission itself the problem?**
A1: No. Commission is a standard remuneration structure. The question is not whether commission exists but **whether the diagnostic stage is independent of the sale**. Test it with question 5 rather than by asking whether they are paid.

**Q2: I'm uncomfortable asking question 5.**
A2: Soften the wording: "I'd like to understand which parts of your recommendation might interact with your own arrangements." **The phrasing can be gentle; the question still deserves to be asked** — it is the most discriminating of the six.

**Q3: My adviser said "you don't need me for this." Are they brushing me off?**
A3: Usually the opposite. An adviser who never says it has every recommendation discounted by one layer of credibility. **Being able to mark out where you are not needed is itself part of the expertise.**

**Q4: My adviser said this — can I now simply trust them?**
A4: **No.** A line can be learned, and it is learned easily. What decides whether it means anything is the precondition behind it — **whether this adviser's diagnosis is independent of the sale**. So the test does not change: do not weigh what they say, weigh which side they took **on the occasion it cost them**. **Once is not a sample.** An adviser who volunteers the disclosure and still recommends closing now has only delivered the setup; an adviser who discloses and then advises against closing has given you behaviour to verify.

**Q5: Does this apply only to insurance?**
A5: No. It applies wherever **the person advising and the person selling are the same person** — insurance, parts of wealth management, some legal and tax services. Insurance is used here because the conflict points are unusually visible.

**Q6: I've already bought. Is this still useful?**
A6: Yes. Questions 6 and 2 apply directly to an existing relationship. **Post-issue service quality says more about the nature of the relationship than pre-sale attentiveness ever did.**
