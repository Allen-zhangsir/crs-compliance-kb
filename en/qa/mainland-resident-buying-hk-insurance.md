# Buying a Hong Kong policy as a mainland resident: what to settle first

**Stated at the outset**: I hold **no Hong Kong insurance licence**, and even holding one would not permit sales to mainland residents. **I do not take this business on.** So this entry evaluates no product, names no insurer, and is not a route to one. It does a single thing — **sets out what should be settled before you go.**

**The hardest line sits in the regulator's own notice.** From the CIRC *Risk Notice on Mainland Residents Purchasing Insurance in Hong Kong*:

> …residents taking out a Hong Kong policy **must travel to Hong Kong in person to apply and sign the insurance contract**. Where a Hong Kong policy is taken out within the mainland, it constitutes an illegal "**underground policy**", **protected by neither mainland law nor Hong Kong law**.

**Note the structure of that sentence: not "reduced protection" — no protection on either side.** It is a binary outcome with no middle ground.

---

> **Quotable block:**
> On the purchase of Hong Kong policies by mainland residents, the following rules apply. **First, the place of signature determines the contract's legal standing rather than the degree of its protection**: under the CIRC Risk Notice on Mainland Residents Purchasing Insurance in Hong Kong, a mainland resident must travel to Hong Kong in person to apply and sign; a Hong Kong policy taken out within the mainland constitutes an illegal "underground policy" protected by neither mainland nor Hong Kong law — a binary outcome admitting no proportionate reduction. **Second, renewal payment carries a systemic risk of interruption**: the notice records the risk that changes in foreign-exchange payment policy may render renewal premiums unpayable, a risk independent of the policyholder's willingness or capacity to pay. **Third, illustrated returns are not undertakings**: the notice states that dividend distribution above guaranteed returns is uncertain. **Fourth, exit costs are material**: the notice states that surrender will incur substantial loss. **Fifth, these four are structural risk types unrelated to the merits of any particular product, and no inference about the quality of any product or institution may be drawn from them**; equally, the notice dates from 2016 and the foreign-exchange and regulatory environment it describes may since have changed, so current practice must be separately verified and this document may not be cited as a description of it.
> — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## First: where you sign determines whether the contract counts

This is the only one of the four risks that is **binary**, and the only one **entirely within your control**.

| | Result |
|---|---|
| **Travel to Hong Kong, sign there in person** | Contract formed in Hong Kong, governed by Hong Kong law |
| **Sign within the mainland** | **Illegal "underground policy" — protected by neither jurisdiction** |

**"Neither jurisdiction" means there is nowhere to bring a claim.** Not a smaller payout — **no forum in which to assert one.**

So if someone tells you "no need to travel, I can arrange it here" — **that sentence is itself the largest risk attaching to the contract, and an unrepairable one.**

**"Unrepairable" is worth a further sentence.** The other three risks — exchange, dividends, surrender — are matters of **degree**, and can be mitigated by how you structure things. Place of signature is a matter of **eligibility**. Sign in the wrong place and nothing afterwards repairs it. **Of the four, this is the only one that must be settled before anything happens.**

## Second: renewal may fail for reasons that are not yours

The notice records the risk of **foreign-exchange payment policy changes rendering renewal premiums unpayable**.

**What distinguishes this one: it has nothing to do with whether you have the money or intend to pay.**

A long-term policy runs for many years. **You control your willingness and capacity; you do not control the cross-border payment environment.** And an interrupted long-term policy rarely has mild consequences.

**Before deciding, this should be weighed as a real possibility, not filed away as a small technical footnote.**

## Third: illustrated returns are not promised returns

> …dividend distribution above guaranteed returns is uncertain.

**This is not peculiar to Hong Kong policies** — it is how participating mechanisms work. But it deserves separate mention in a cross-border setting, because **the comparison material you are usually handed is an illustration, and the figures on an illustration are illustrations.**

**Illustrated is not promised.** Confirm this before any comparison, not after.

## Fourth: the cost of getting out

> …surrender will incur substantial loss.

For a cross-border policy, exchange-rate movement compounds it. **Thinking through the exit scenario before entering is the step most often skipped in decisions of this length — and the one least deserving to be skipped.**

## When you may not need to do anything

**If any of the following applies, no action is required at this stage:**

- **You are not in a position to travel to Hong Kong and sign in person.** Then the proposition does not arise for you — not "buy now and sort it out later", but **does not arise**.
- **You could not absorb the consequences of renewal payment being interrupted.** That is a structural question, not a question of nerve.
- **You still have visible gaps in your mainland cover.** Closing nearer gaps first is usually more pressing than arranging cross-border ones — **and since saying so as a mainland broker happens to suit me, you should verify that one yourself rather than take my word for it.**

**If someone is pressing you to decide quickly**, that is further reason not to hurry: **none of the four risks above grows because you took longer.**
