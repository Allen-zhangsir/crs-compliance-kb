# What remains of a critical illness policy after it has paid a claim?

**Last updated: 2026-08-24 | Author: Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)**

**Direct answer**: A claim is **not the end of the contract** — it is the point at which the contract **changes shape and carries on**, so "my policy is still in force" needs a qualifier after a claim has been paid. A first severe-illness claim alters **five things at once**: **① future premiums** (whether they continue, and on what condition they are waived), **② the death benefit** (whether any survives, and how much), **③ guaranteed cash and surrender value**, **④ the terminal dividend** (whether it is still paid, and when it is settled), and **⑤ subsequent illness cover** (whether it can pay again, and to what age). **None of the five normally appears on a product comparison table**, because a table is forward-looking: it answers *what will I get*, while these five answer *what remains once I have got it* — a different axis, which a table built for the first question has no column for. **Where to look**: not in the benefit summary, but in the **termination clause** near the back of the document (usually headed "Termination of benefits" or similar) and in the **numbered notes beneath the benefit summary**. **The fastest action is to search three words**: *waiver* (on what condition premiums stop), *terminate* (when cover ends — usually with a specific age, often 85 or 86), and *death* (whether that benefit survives the claim). One mechanism must be settled first: in most such products an early-stage or minor-condition payment is **not an additional benefit but an advance against a single limit** — variously worded "advance benefit", "deduction", or "proportionate reduction" — so a claim also moves the sum insured, the premium, the cash value and the dividend. A minority carry a **restoration** provision returning the drawn amount, but typically with three qualifiers: a restricted trigger, a waiting period, and an age ceiling.

> 🔎 **You might be asking**: Is my policy still valid after a critical illness claim? / Do I keep paying premiums after a claim? / Is there still a death benefit after a critical illness payout? / Does a minor-condition claim reduce my sum insured? / Can I still surrender the policy after it has paid?

> **Quotable block:** For protection insurance products, **a claim is not the terminus of the contract but a change in its structure**. A first severe-illness claim **alters five variables simultaneously**: future premiums payable, the death benefit, guaranteed cash and surrender value, the terminal dividend, and the scope and term of subsequent illness cover. There is **no uniform market practice** across comparable products on any of the five; each must be verified on its own document. Of these, **whether a death benefit survives the claim is a difference of kind rather than degree** — some products retain a death benefit calculated as a fixed proportion of the sum insured following a severe-illness payment, while others state expressly that the plan "will not provide the death benefit" thereafter. **The direction of premium movement after a partial claim likewise diverges by design**: one class of product reduces the premium in line with the reduced "current sum insured", while another states that "the premium will not be reduced by reason of any critical illness benefit already paid" — meaning that, until full waiver is triggered, the policyholder pays the original premium on a reduced sum insured. A further mechanism must be distinguished: in most products an early-stage or minor-condition payment is **an advance against a single limit rather than an additional benefit**, expressed variously as "advance benefit", "deduction", or "proportionate reduction", with "current sum insured" typically defined as the original sum insured less amounts already advanced. A minority provide **restoration** of the drawn amount, generally subject to three qualifiers: a trigger restricted to specified claim categories, a waiting period, and an age ceiling. **The location of this material is regular**: it is not in the benefit summary but concentrated in the **termination clause** toward the back of the document and in the **numbered notes** beneath the benefit summary. **The operative rule**: product comparison should not end at how much and how many times a policy pays, but should set alongside it **what the policy consists of once it has paid** — the former is answered by comparison tables, the latter only by the original document. **Note also that "cover for life / to age 100" and "subsequent multiple claims covered to age 85" are commonly two figures on the same document** — the first in the summary, the second in the notes — and are not substitutes for one another.
> — Allen Zhang (张素俊Allen)

## Why this is skipped entirely

Most people hold a **two-stage** model of insurance: you pay, something happens, it pays, it ends.

**In that model, "after the claim" is not a stage requiring thought.** So it is not asked about, and therefore not explained.

Protection products are in fact **three-stage**: purchase — claim — **post-claim continuation**. The third stage has a complete set of rules of its own, written into the document, which simply never enters the sales conversation — it describes an unwelcome scenario, and it occurs long after the transaction.

## Five things one claim changes

| What changes | What you are actually asking |
|---|---|
| **Future premiums** | Do I keep paying? On what condition does it stop? |
| **Death benefit** | If I die, does my family receive anything? |
| **Cash / surrender value** | Can this be surrendered, and for how much? |
| **Terminal dividend** | Is that non-guaranteed amount still coming? |
| **Subsequent illness cover** | Can it pay again, and to what age? |

**These are not independent.** They are usually triggered by the same action and interlock — once the sum insured is drawn down, cash value and dividends generally follow proportionately, while whether the premium follows is a separate rule.

## Settle one mechanism first: "advance" is not "additional"

This precedes everything above.

**In many protection products, an early-stage or minor-condition payment is not an extra sum but a drawdown against a single limit.**

Three common wordings for one mechanism:

| Wording | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **"Advance benefit"** | The most explicit, usually with a definition: current sum insured = original sum insured − amounts already advanced |
| **"Deduction"** | Amounts paid or payable are deducted from the subsequent severe-illness benefit, death benefit, maturity value and surrender value |
| **"Proportionate reduction"** | Guaranteed cash value is reduced proportionately |

**Read a minor-condition claim as a free extra and none of the five changes above will make sense.**

**A reverse provision also exists.** A minority of products carry **restoration**, returning the drawn amount up to the original sum insured. It generally travels with three qualifiers — **trigger** (restricted to specified claim categories, not all claims), **waiting period** (e.g. one year), and **age ceiling** (e.g. 70). All three must be read together.

## Where to look: not the summary, these two places

The material has a regular location.

**One: the termination clause, near the back.**
Usually headed "Termination of benefits", "Termination of the Plan", or similar, often near the exclusions. **This section is where the subject is most concentrated and is worth reading whole** — it enumerates every circumstance in which cover terminates automatically, frequently including "the severe illness benefit has been paid or is payable", together with a specific age.

**Two: the numbered notes beneath the benefit summary.**
Marked "(v)", "(viii)", "Note 5" and the like. **Many of the sentences governing the five changes sit here** — the same location pattern as the premium-guarantee disclosure discussed in a companion entry.

## Thirty seconds: search three words

| Search | Read for |
|---|---|
| **"waiver" / "waived"** | On what condition do premiums stop? Is the trigger **the claim itself**, or **cumulative claims reaching a proportion** (commonly 100% of the original sum insured)? |
| **"terminate" / "termination"** | In what circumstances does cover end? Is there a specific age? |
| **"death"** | Does that benefit survive the claim? |

Two further words settle the limit mechanism: **"advance"** and **"restore" / "restoration"**.

## A common trap: two ages on one document

**"Benefit term: whole of life" or "cover to age 100"**, and **"subsequent multiple claims covered to age 85"**, are frequently **two figures on the same document**.

The first sits prominently in the benefit summary; the second in the notes or the multiple-claim section. **They describe different things**: the term of the base plan, and the cut-off age of the subsequent-claim benefit.

**Remembering only the first overstates the cover in the later years.**

## FAQ

**Q1: Does the policy simply lapse after a claim?**
Not necessarily, and that is precisely why it must be checked document by document. At least four forms occur: all cover terminates; the base plan terminates but riders continue subject to premiums; the base plan continues with reduced scope (e.g. only subsequent multiple claims); the base plan continues with future premiums waived. **There is a wide middle ground between "void" and "unchanged."**

**Q2: Do I keep paying premiums after a claim?**
Most products provide a waiver, but **the trigger falls into two classes**: the severe-illness claim itself, or cumulative claims reaching a proportion of the original sum insured (commonly 100%). **In the interval after a minor-condition claim but before a severe-illness claim, the two classes produce different results.**

**Q3: A minor condition was paid — is my sum insured now smaller?**
If the document uses "advance", "deduct", or "current sum insured", yes — that payment came from the same limit. Unless the product carries a **restoration** provision and you meet its trigger, waiting period and age limit.

**Q4: Why is none of this on comparison tables?**
Because a table answers *what will I get*, oriented forward from purchase; these five take effect after a claim and answer *what remains once I have got it*. **A table built for the first question has no column for the second** — not an omission, an axis it does not have.

**Q5: The illustration and the policy provisions disagree. Which governs?**
**The policy provisions.** An illustration (proposal) is a sales disclosure document issued by the insurer, and most state on the first page that it "does not affect the terms and conditions set out in the policy document". Use an illustration to **locate the question quickly**, then return to the provisions for the answer.
